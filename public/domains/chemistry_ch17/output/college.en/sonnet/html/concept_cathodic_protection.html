Cathodic Protection

Metal corrosion is fundamentally an electrochemical process: at anodic sites on a metal surface, atoms lose electrons and dissolve into solution as ions ($\text{Fe} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+} + 2e^-$), while at cathodic sites those electrons are consumed by a reduction reaction, commonly oxygen combining with water ($\text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 4e^- \rightarrow 4\text{OH}^-$). Cathodic protection is an engineering technique that stops this loss of metal by forcing the entire protected structure to behave as a cathode, so that oxidation happens somewhere else instead.

There are two standard ways to achieve this. The first, sacrificial anode protection, attaches a more reactive metal — commonly zinc, magnesium, or aluminum — to the structure being protected (steel pipelines, ship hulls, underground tanks). Because the sacrificial metal is higher on the reactivity series, it gives up electrons more readily than iron does, so it corrodes preferentially while feeding electrons to the steel and keeping it cathodic. The anode is eventually consumed and must be replaced, hence "sacrificial." The second method, impressed current cathodic protection (ICCP), uses an external DC power source connected to an inert or slowly consumed anode (graphite, mixed-metal oxide, or high-silicon iron) buried or immersed near the structure. The power supply continuously pushes electrons onto the structure, again holding it at cathodic potential, and can be tuned for structures too large for sacrificial anodes alone, such as long pipelines or offshore platforms.

Choosing between these two methods is a genuine engineering problem. Suppose a company must protect a buried steel pipeline 50 km long in soil with moderate resistivity. Sacrificial anodes are simple, need no external power, and are ideal for smaller or remote structures, but their limited driving voltage means each anode only protects a modest radius, requiring many anodes strung along the line — costly to install and maintain over such a distance. ICCP delivers far greater current and can protect long distances from a few power stations, but it requires monitoring instrumentation, a power supply, and rectifier maintenance, and can accidentally cause "stray current corrosion" of nearby unprotected metal if the applied voltage is too high. In practice, engineers measure a structure's baseline potential with a reference electrode, compare it against the standard criterion of roughly $-850\ \text{mV}$ (vs. copper/copper-sulfate electrode) for adequately protected steel, and use ICCP for long or large structures, reserving sacrificial anodes for compact, low-current-demand applications like ship hulls or small storage tanks.
