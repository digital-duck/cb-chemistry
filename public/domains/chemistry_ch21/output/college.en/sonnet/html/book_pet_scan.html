Concept Book: Pet Scan

Chemistry Ch21 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

A PET scan is the point where everything the book has built — radioactive decay, tracer biochemistry, coincidence detection, and tomographic reconstruction — stops being separate ideas and becomes a single instrument for seeing metabolism in a living body. Injecting a small dose of a positron-emitting tracer such as fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) lets a scanner detect the back-to-back gamma photons produced when an emitted positron annihilates with a nearby electron. By recording thousands of these coincident photon pairs from many angles and reconstructing them into a three-dimensional image, a PET scan does not just show anatomy, as an X-ray or CT scan does — it shows function: which tissues are consuming glucose fastest, which regions are most metabolically active right now. That shift from structure to activity is why PET is a natural capstone: it requires nuclear physics to produce the tracer, chemistry to bind it to a biologically relevant molecule, detector physics to capture the signal, and computational reconstruction to turn raw counts into an image — the full arc of the concepts preceding it, applied at once.

This capability radiates into several domains. In oncology, PET reveals tumors as hotspots of abnormal glucose uptake, often before a CT scan shows any size change, and it distinguishes active cancer from scar tissue after treatment. In neurology, PET tracers targeting amyloid plaques or dopamine receptors let clinicians visualize the biochemical signatures of Alzheimer's disease or Parkinson's disease years before symptoms fully manifest. In cardiology, PET measures blood flow and viable heart muscle, guiding decisions about bypass surgery versus medical management. In pharmaceutical research, PET tracers track how an experimental drug distributes through the body and whether it reaches its intended target, accelerating early-phase trials.
