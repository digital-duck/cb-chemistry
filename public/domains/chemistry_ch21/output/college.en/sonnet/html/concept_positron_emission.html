Positron Emission

Positron emission (also called β⁺ decay) is a mode of radioactive decay in which a proton inside an unstable nucleus converts into a neutron, releasing a positron (the antimatter counterpart of the electron) and a neutrino. The general reaction is written as

$$ ^{A}_{Z}X \;\rightarrow\; ^{A}_{\;\,Z-1}Y \;+\; e^+ \;+\; \nu_e $$

Because a proton becomes a neutron, the mass number $A$ stays the same, but the atomic number $Z$ decreases by one — the daughter nucleus sits one place earlier in the periodic table. This is the mirror image of β⁻ decay, where a neutron converts into a proton instead. Positron emission occurs in nuclei that have too many protons relative to neutrons to be stable, and it happens only when the parent-daughter mass difference is large enough to supply the energy needed to create the positron and to cover the daughter atom's extra electron.

Worked example. Carbon-11 decays by positron emission to boron-11:

$$ ^{11}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow \,^{11}_{5}\text{B} + e^+ + \nu_e $$

Carbon has 6 protons and boron has 5, confirming the proton-to-neutron conversion. Carbon-11 is a proton-rich isotope produced in cyclotrons for exactly this decay behavior.

Problem-solving application. Positron emission is the physical basis of PET (positron emission tomography) imaging. A radioactive tracer such as $^{18}$F is attached to a biologically active molecule (e.g., fluorodeoxyglucose) and injected into a patient. When the tracer decays inside the body, the emitted positron travels only a fraction of a millimeter before it meets an ordinary electron. The two particles disappear, and their mass reappears as two flashes of radiation that shoot off in opposite directions. A ring of detectors around the patient catches these paired flashes: whenever two detectors on opposite sides fire at essentially the same instant, the decay must have happened somewhere on the straight line connecting them. By collecting many such lines from decays throughout the body, doctors can pinpoint where the tracer — and therefore the metabolic activity it marks — is concentrated, which is how PET scans locate tumors and other regions of abnormal cell activity. This example shows how a single nuclear decay process, once understood at the level of individual protons and neutrons, can be engineered into a practical tool for seeing inside a living body.
