Pet Scan

Definition

A positron emission tomography (PET) scan is a medical imaging technique that reveals how tissues function, not just how they look. A patient is injected with a small amount of a radioactive tracer—most commonly fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG), a glucose molecule tagged with a radioactive fluorine isotope. Metabolically active tissue, such as the brain or a fast-growing tumor, absorbs more glucose and therefore accumulates more tracer. As the isotope decays, it emits a positron, which almost immediately collides with a nearby electron. This collision annihilates both particles and produces two gamma-ray photons that shoot off in nearly opposite directions. A ring of detectors surrounding the patient captures these paired photons; a computer identifies pairs arriving at nearly the same instant (coincidence detection) and reconstructs the location of each annihilation event, building a 3-D map of tracer concentration throughout the body.

Worked example

Suppose two detectors on opposite sides of a scanner ring register gamma photons 0.3 nanoseconds apart, while the ring's coincidence-timing window is set to 0.5 nanoseconds. Because $0.3\,\text{ns} < 0.5\,\text{ns}$, the system accepts this as a true coincidence event and logs a line connecting the two detectors—one "vote" that an annihilation occurred somewhere along that line. Repeating this over millions of photon pairs from many angles lets reconstruction software (using back-projection algorithms similar in principle to CT reconstruction) triangulate the tracer's distribution, producing an image where "hot spots" mark regions of elevated metabolic activity, such as a malignant tumor.

Problem-solving application

PET scanning is often combined with CT or MRI (PET/CT, PET/MRI) so that the metabolic hot spot from PET can be overlaid on the precise anatomical detail from the other modality, telling clinicians not just that something is metabolically abnormal but where it is. This fusion is central to oncology (locating tumors and checking whether treatment has reduced their activity), cardiology (assessing blood flow to heart muscle), and neurology (distinguishing Alzheimer's-related metabolic decline from normal aging). When evaluating a PET result, the key reasoning skill is distinguishing signal from noise: not every area of elevated glucose uptake is cancer—inflammation and normal high-metabolism organs (brain, heart, bladder) also light up, so clinicians must interpret intensity, location, and shape together rather than reading the image in isolation.
