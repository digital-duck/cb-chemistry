Band Theory

When atoms bond together to form a solid, their individual, sharply defined electron energy levels merge and spread into continuous ranges called energy bands. In an isolated atom, electrons occupy discrete orbitals with specific energies. Bring $10^{23}$ atoms together into a crystal lattice, and the overlapping electron wavefunctions force those once-identical energy levels to split slightly, packing so closely together that they form a quasi-continuous band of allowed energies. Between these bands lie forbidden energy ranges called band gaps, where no electron states exist. The two most important bands are the valence band (the highest band that is normally filled with electrons) and the conduction band (the next band up, where electrons can move freely and carry current).

Whether a material conducts electricity, insulates, or does something in between depends entirely on how these bands are arranged and filled. In a conductor like copper, the valence and conduction bands overlap, or the valence band is only partially filled, so electrons can move into nearby empty states with almost no energy cost — current flows easily. In an insulator like diamond, the valence band is completely full and separated from the conduction band by a large gap (about 5.5 eV), so electrons have essentially no way to jump across at room temperature. A semiconductor like silicon has the same basic structure as an insulator, but with a much smaller gap (about 1.1 eV) — small enough that heat, light, or an applied voltage can kick a meaningful number of electrons across it.

This framework lets us solve practical problems. Suppose you're told a material has a band gap of 0.1 eV and you want to know whether it conducts at room temperature. Thermal energy at 300 K is roughly $k_BT \approx 0.026$ eV. Since the gap is only about four times $k_BT$, a non-negligible fraction of electrons will be thermally excited into the conduction band — the material behaves as a semiconductor, not an insulator, even though its gap is nonzero. This same reasoning explains why silicon solar cells and LEDs are engineered around specific band gaps: a smaller gap captures more sunlight (lower-energy photons) but leaks more current as heat, while an LED's gap size directly sets the color of light it emits, since photon energy equals the gap energy, $E_{photon} = E_g$.
