Crystal Field Theory

When a transition-metal ion sits at the center of a complex, it is surrounded by ligands—molecules or ions like $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{NH}_3$, or $\text{Cl}^-$ that donate electron pairs to the metal. Crystal field theory (CFT) explains what happens to the metal's five $d$ orbitals when this electrostatic environment is imposed on them. In an isolated ion, all five $d$ orbitals have identical energy (they are "degenerate"). But ligands are not spread uniformly in space—they approach along specific directions, typically the $x$, $y$, and $z$ axes in an octahedral complex. Orbitals that point directly at the incoming ligands ($d_{x^2-y^2}$ and $d_{z^2}$) experience stronger electron-electron repulsion than orbitals that point between the ligands ($d_{xy}$, $d_{xz}$, $d_{yz}$). This splits the five orbitals into two energy groups: a higher-energy pair ($e_g$) and a lower-energy triplet ($t_{2g}$), separated by an energy gap called $\Delta_o$ (the octahedral splitting energy).

Consider $[\text{Ti}(\text{H}_2\text{O})_6]^{3+}$, which has one $d$ electron. That electron occupies the lower $t_{2g}$ level rather than being spread evenly, because doing so lowers the ion's total energy. When light strikes the complex, the electron can absorb a photon and jump from $t_{2g}$ to $e_g$—and the energy of that photon equals $\Delta_o$. Because $\Delta_o$ falls in the visible range, the complex absorbs a specific color of light and appears colored (violet, in this case) as the complement of the absorbed wavelength.

This model lets you predict and explain real chemical behavior. Given an ion's $d$-electron count and whether ligands are "strong-field" (like $\text{CN}^-$, which produces a large $\Delta_o$) or "weak-field" (like $\text{Cl}^-$, small $\Delta_o$), you can determine whether electrons pair up in the lower orbitals (low-spin) or spread out singly across both levels (high-spin) before pairing, following Hund's rule modified by $\Delta_o$. For a $d^6$ ion like $\text{Fe}^{2+}$, a strong-field ligand forces all six electrons into $t_{2g}$ (low-spin, diamagnetic or weakly paramagnetic), while a weak-field ligand keeps four electrons unpaired (high-spin, strongly paramagnetic). This directly predicts magnetic behavior and helps explain why hemoglobin's iron center changes properties when it binds oxygen versus when it doesn't.
