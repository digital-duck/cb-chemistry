Ionic Radius

Ionic radius is the effective distance from an ion's nucleus to the outer edge of its electron cloud. Because ions are charged, their radii differ systematically from the radii of their neutral parent atoms. Losing electrons to form a cation shrinks the ion: fewer electrons mean less electron-electron repulsion, and the remaining electrons are pulled in more tightly by the same nuclear charge. Gaining electrons to form an anion does the opposite — more electrons increase repulsion and spread the cloud outward, so anions are larger than their parent atoms. Within an isoelectronic series (ions with the same number of electrons), radius decreases as nuclear charge increases, since more protons pull the same electron count in more tightly.

Worked example. Compare $\text{Na}$, $\text{Na}^+$, and $\text{F}^-$. Neutral sodium has 11 electrons in three shells; removing one electron to form $\text{Na}^+$ leaves only two filled shells (10 electrons, same configuration as neon), so $\text{Na}^+$ ($\approx 102$ pm) is much smaller than $\text{Na}$ ($\approx 186$ pm). Now compare $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{F}^-$: both have exactly 10 electrons (isoelectronic), but $\text{Na}^+$ has 11 protons while $\text{F}^-$ has only 9. The stronger nuclear pull in $\text{Na}^+$ makes it smaller ($\approx 102$ pm) than $\text{F}^-$ ($\approx 133$ pm), even though $\text{F}^-$ started as a smaller neutral atom.

Problem-solving application. Ionic radius trends let you predict properties without memorizing tables. Suppose you need to rank $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{Na}^+$, and $\text{F}^-$ by size. First check electron count: all three are isoelectronic with neon (10 electrons), so the only variable is nuclear charge. Proton counts are 12, 11, and 9, respectively. More protons means tighter binding and a smaller ion, so the order from smallest to largest is $\text{Mg}^{2+} < \text{Na}^+ < \text{F}^-$. This same reasoning explains why ionic compounds pack the way they do in crystal lattices: lattice energy depends on the sum of the cation and anion radii (through Coulomb's law, since force scales as $1/r^2$), so a smaller cation paired with a smaller anion produces a more tightly bound, higher-melting solid. Ionic radius trends are therefore a practical shortcut for predicting lattice energies, solubility patterns, and even ion mobility through biological membranes, where size determines whether an ion fits through a given channel.
