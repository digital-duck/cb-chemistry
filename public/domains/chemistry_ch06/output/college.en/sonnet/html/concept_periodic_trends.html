Periodic Trends

Periodic trends are the systematic patterns in atomic properties—atomic radius, ionization energy, electron affinity, and electronegativity—that emerge as you move across a row or down a column of the periodic table. These patterns exist because two competing factors change predictably with an element's position: the number of protons in the nucleus (nuclear charge) and the number of electron shells (principal energy levels) surrounding it. Rather than memorizing values for each of 118 elements, chemists use these trends to predict and explain behavior.

Consider atomic radius. Moving left to right across a period, radius shrinks, even though electrons are being added. Why? Protons are also being added, and each new electron enters the same outer shell as its neighbors. The added electrons don't shield each other effectively from the growing positive charge, so the effective nuclear charge (the net pull felt by outer electrons) increases, tugging electrons inward. Moving down a group, radius increases because each row adds an entirely new shell, and shielding from inner electrons outweighs the modest increase in nuclear charge.

Ionization energy—the energy needed to remove an outermost electron—follows the inverse logic: it rises across a period (electrons are held more tightly) and falls down a group (outer electrons are farther away and more shielded, so easier to remove).

Worked example: Rank Na, Mg, and K by first ionization energy. Na and Mg are in the same period; Mg has one more proton and a smaller radius, so Mg's ionization energy is higher than Na's. Na and K are in the same group; K's outer electron is one shell farther out, so it's easier to remove—K has the lowest ionization energy. Order: K < Na < Mg.

Problem-solving application: Trends let you predict reactivity without a data table. An element with low ionization energy (bottom-left of the table, like K or Cs) readily loses an electron, making it a strong reducing agent. An element with high electronegativity and small radius (top-right, like F or O) strongly attracts electrons, making it a strong oxidizing agent. This is why alkali metals react violently with halogens: one side gives up electrons easily, the other demands them—predictable purely from position on the table.
