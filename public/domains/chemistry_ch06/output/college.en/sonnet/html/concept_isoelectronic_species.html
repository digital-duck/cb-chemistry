Isoelectronic Species

Isoelectronic species are atoms, ions, or molecules that have the same number of valence electrons and, for molecules, the same number of atoms arranged in the same bonding pattern. The term comes from "iso" (same) and "electronic" (electron count), and it is one of the most useful shortcuts in chemistry because species with matching electron counts and connectivity tend to share similar geometry, bonding character, and even reactivity — even when their constituent atoms are completely different.

Consider $\text{N}_2$, $\text{CO}$, and $\text{CN}^-$. Nitrogen gas has 14 electrons total (7 from each N atom). Carbon monoxide has 6 (from C) + 8 (from O) = 14 electrons. The cyanide ion has 6 (from C) + 7 (from N) + 1 (extra electron for the negative charge) = 14 electrons. All three are diatomic, all three have 14 electrons, and all three form a triple bond with one lone pair on each terminal atom. This is why CO binds to hemoglobin's iron almost as strongly as $\text{O}_2$ does — its bonding orbitals and electron distribution closely mimic those of $\text{N}_2$, giving it comparable size, bond strength, and lone-pair geometry for coordinating a metal center.

The same logic extends to simple ions: $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{F}^-$, $\text{O}^{2-}$, and neutral Ne are isoelectronic, each having 10 electrons and the same electron configuration ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^6$). Recognizing this lets you predict relative ionic sizes without memorizing a table: among isoelectronic species, higher nuclear charge pulls the shared electron cloud in tighter, so ionic radius shrinks as atomic number increases — $\text{O}^{2-} > \text{F}^- > \text{Ne} > \text{Na}^+ > \text{Mg}^{2+}$.

To apply this in practice, first count valence (or total) electrons and atoms for the species in question, then compare against a known reference species with established structure and properties. For example, given $\text{NO}^+$, count 5 (N) + 6 (O) − 1 (positive charge) = 10 valence electrons in a diatomic arrangement — matching $\text{N}_2$'s valence electron count and predicting a similar triple-bond structure and short, strong bond length. This electron-counting strategy is a fast, reliable way to infer structure and bonding for unfamiliar species by anchoring them to ones you already understand.
