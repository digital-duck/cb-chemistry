Covalent Radius

The covalent radius of an atom is defined as half the distance between the nuclei of two identical atoms joined by a single covalent bond. Because electrons are not confined to sharp boundaries, an atom does not have one true "size"—but the covalent radius gives chemists a consistent, measurable way to assign each element an effective radius, derived from bond lengths observed in real molecules. These values are tabulated for nearly every element and are additive to a good approximation: the length of a bond between two different atoms is often close to the sum of their individual covalent radii.

Consider the $\text{Cl}_2$ molecule. Experimentally, the distance between the two chlorine nuclei is measured (by X-ray diffraction) to be $198\text{ pm}$. Since the bond joins two identical atoms, the covalent radius of chlorine is simply half that distance: $99\text{ pm}$. Apply the same logic to $\text{H}_2$, whose bond length is $74\text{ pm}$, giving hydrogen a covalent radius of $37\text{ pm}$.

The real payoff of covalent radii is predictive: once you know the radius of each element, you can estimate bond lengths in molecules you have never measured. Suppose you want to predict the length of the H–Cl bond. Using additivity,

$$ r_{\text{H–Cl}} \approx r_H + r_{Cl} = 37\text{ pm} + 99\text{ pm} = 136\text{ pm}. $$

The experimental value is $127\text{ pm}$—close, though not exact, because additivity ignores differences in electronegativity, bond order, and hybridization. This discrepancy is itself useful: chemists often interpret a bond that is shorter than the additive prediction as evidence of partial ionic character or multiple-bond behavior, since more strongly attracted or more highly bonded atoms tend to pull closer together than radius addition alone predicts.

Covalent radii also let you compare periodic trends efficiently. Radii shrink across a period (left to right) because added protons increase nuclear charge without adding new electron shells, pulling bonding electrons in tighter. Radii grow down a group because each successive row adds a new occupied shell farther from the nucleus. A student equipped with a table of covalent radii can therefore estimate an unfamiliar bond length, sanity-check reported experimental values, and reason about which trends in molecular geometry or reactivity should follow from atomic size alone.
