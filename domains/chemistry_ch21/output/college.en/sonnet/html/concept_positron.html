Positron

A positron is the antiparticle of the electron: it carries the same mass and spin as an electron but an opposite charge, $+e$ instead of $-e$. It was predicted mathematically before it was ever observed. In 1928, Paul Dirac wrote down a relativistic wave equation for the electron that, when solved, admitted two families of energy solutions — one positive, one negative. Rather than discard the negative-energy branch as unphysical, Dirac interpreted it as evidence for a new particle with the electron's mass but positive charge. Carl Anderson confirmed this experimentally in 1932, observing tracks in a cloud chamber that curved the "wrong" way for an electron in a magnetic field, revealing the positron.

Positrons matter because when a positron meets an electron, the two annihilate, converting their combined rest mass entirely into energy, released as two gamma-ray photons. This is a direct, testable consequence of Einstein's mass-energy relation, $E = mc^2$. For a single electron-positron pair, each particle has rest mass $m_e \approx 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$, so the total energy released is

$$ E = 2 m_e c^2 \approx 2(9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg})(3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s})^2 \approx 1.64 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J} \approx 1.02\ \text{MeV}. $$

By conservation of momentum, if the pair annihilates at rest, the two resulting photons must travel in exactly opposite directions, each carrying about 0.511 MeV.

This principle underlies Positron Emission Tomography (PET), a medical imaging technique. A patient is injected with a radioactive tracer that undergoes positron emission (a form of beta-plus decay). Each emitted positron travels a short distance, annihilates with a nearby electron, and produces two oppositely directed 0.511 MeV photons. Detectors arranged in a ring around the patient register these paired photons arriving in near-simultaneous "coincidence." Because the two photons travel in opposite directions along a straight line, the detector pair that registers them defines a line along which the annihilation occurred. Combining thousands of such lines lets a computer reconstruct a 3D map of where the tracer concentrated — revealing, for example, regions of high metabolic activity such as tumors. The problem-solving skill here is working backward from a conservation law (momentum, energy) to infer an unobservable event's location from observable byproducts.
