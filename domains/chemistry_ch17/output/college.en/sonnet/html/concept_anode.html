Anode

An anode is the electrode in an electrochemical cell or electronic device at which oxidation occurs — the loss of electrons. This definition holds regardless of whether the cell generates electricity (a galvanic or voltaic cell, like a battery) or consumes it (an electrolytic cell, like one used for electroplating). The common thread is chemical, not electrical: wherever a species gives up electrons, that electrode is the anode. Its counterpart, the cathode, is where reduction — electron gain — occurs.

A frequent point of confusion is polarity. In a discharging battery, the anode is negative because it is the source of electrons flowing into the external circuit. In an electrolytic cell, where an external power source forces a non-spontaneous reaction, the anode is connected to the positive terminal of that source. Students should anchor their reasoning in the chemistry (oxidation happens here) rather than memorizing a sign, which flips depending on cell type.

Worked example. Consider a zinc–copper galvanic cell (a Daniell cell). At the zinc electrode, metallic zinc loses electrons:

$$\text{Zn}(s) \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^-$$

This is oxidation, so the zinc electrode is the anode. Those electrons travel through an external wire to the copper electrode, where copper ions are reduced:

$$\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}(s)$$

The copper electrode, undergoing reduction, is the cathode. Adding the two half-reactions gives the overall spontaneous cell reaction, $\text{Zn}(s) + \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)$, which is the source of the cell's voltage.

Problem-solving application. Given an unfamiliar redox pair, identify the anode by determining which species has a more negative (or less positive) standard reduction potential — that species will instead undergo oxidation. For example, given $\text{Fe}^{3+}/\text{Fe}^{2+}$ ($E^\circ = +0.77\,\text{V}$) paired with $\text{Sn}^{4+}/\text{Sn}^{2+}$ ($E^\circ = +0.15\,\text{V}$), tin has the lower reduction potential, so it is oxidized: the tin electrode is the anode, and iron is reduced at the cathode. This systematic approach — compare potentials, assign oxidation to the lower one — lets you predict anode identity in any new cell rather than relying on memorized examples.
