Concept Book: Corrosion

Chemistry Ch17 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Corrosion is the electrochemical destruction of a metal through oxidation-reduction reactions with its environment, most commonly the rusting of iron in the presence of oxygen and water. It is the natural endpoint of this concept-book because it forces the synthesis of everything covered so far: oxidation states, half-reactions, standard reduction potentials, and the direction spontaneous electron transfer takes. Corrosion is what happens when a metal, left alone, follows thermodynamics back toward its more stable, oxidized ore-like state. Iron does not simply "wear out" — it reacts. At an anodic site on the metal surface, iron loses electrons: $\text{Fe} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+} + 2e^-$. Those electrons travel through the metal to a cathodic site, where they reduce oxygen dissolved in surface moisture: $\text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 4e^- \rightarrow 4\text{OH}^-$. The $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ and $\text{OH}^-$ combine and further oxidize to form hydrated iron(III) oxide — rust. Because iron's reduction potential ($E^\circ = -0.44\text{ V}$) is less positive than oxygen's ($E^\circ = +0.40\text{ V}$), the overall cell potential is positive, and the reaction proceeds spontaneously wherever metal, water, and air meet.

This is precisely the same architecture as a galvanic cell studied earlier, except the "wiring" is the metal itself rather than an external circuit. That recognition unlocks two protective strategies. Galvanization coats steel with zinc, a metal with an even more negative reduction potential ($E^\circ = -0.76\text{ V}$) than iron. If the coating is scratched, zinc — not iron — becomes the anode and sacrifices itself, since it oxidizes more readily. Cathodic protection generalizes this idea to large structures like pipelines and ship hulls: rather than coating the metal, engineers attach a sacrificial anode (often zinc or magnesium) or apply an external current, deliberately forcing the structure to behave as the cathode, so it is protected rather than consumed.

Both applications answer the same underlying question: given a metal that wants to oxidize, how do you redirect the electron flow so something else oxidizes instead? To see this reasoning applied at industrial scale — where engineers must calculate anode lifespan, current requirements, and replacement schedules — explore cathodic protection next.
