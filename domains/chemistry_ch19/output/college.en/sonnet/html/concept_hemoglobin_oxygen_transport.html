Hemoglobin Oxygen Transport

Hemoglobin is the iron-containing protein in red blood cells that binds oxygen in the lungs, carries it through the bloodstream, and releases it in tissues where it is needed for cellular respiration. Each hemoglobin molecule has four subunits, and each subunit contains a heme group with an iron atom capable of binding one oxygen molecule — so a single hemoglobin can carry up to four O2 molecules at once. The defining feature of hemoglobin is cooperative binding: when one oxygen molecule attaches to a subunit, it changes the protein's shape slightly, making it easier for the next oxygen molecule to bind. This is why hemoglobin's oxygen-binding curve is S-shaped (sigmoidal) rather than a simple rising line — it stays relatively flat at low oxygen levels, then rises steeply, then levels off again near saturation.

Worked example. Consider two locations in the body: the lungs, where the partial pressure of oxygen (\(P_{O_2}\)) is about 100 mmHg, and actively exercising muscle tissue, where \(P_{O_2}\) drops to around 20 mmHg. At 100 mmHg, hemoglobin is roughly 97–98% saturated — nearly full. At 20 mmHg, saturation falls to about 30–35%. That difference — from ~97% down to ~30% — represents the fraction of bound oxygen that gets unloaded into the tissue. This steep drop between the "loading" pressure in the lungs and the "unloading" pressure in tissues is precisely why the curve's S-shape matters: on the steep middle portion of the curve, small changes in tissue oxygen demand trigger large changes in oxygen release, giving the body a sensitive and efficient delivery system.

Problem-solving application. Suppose a patient's tissues are more acidic than normal (as happens during intense exercise, when muscles produce lactic acid and CO2). Lower pH and higher CO2 shift the entire saturation curve to the right — known as the Bohr effect — meaning hemoglobin releases oxygen more readily at any given \(P_{O_2}\). If you're asked to predict oxygen delivery in a scenario involving fever, acidosis, or heavy exercise, the correct approach is: identify whether the curve shifts right (more O2 unloaded, favors delivery to hungry tissue) or left (hemoglobin holds oxygen tighter, as with fetal hemoglobin or high pH), then reason about saturation values at the relevant tissue \(P_{O_2}\) before and after the shift.
